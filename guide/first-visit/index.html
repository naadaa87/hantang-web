FIRST VISIT

Your first tang, step by step

The whole visit in order, from the shoe locker to the checkout counter, with what to say and what to do at the three moments where first-timers freeze.

9 MIN READ · Updated 2026-10-30

A Korean bathhouse visit follows the same basic order: shoes off, pay, undress completely, wash sitting down, soak, sweat, rinse, dress. If the place also has a jjimjilbang (찜질방), a clothed, mixed-gender hall of heated rooms, that comes after the bath, in a cotton outfit the counter gives you. None of it is difficult. The difficulty is that nobody explains the order, and the signs are in Korean.

This guide walks the visit in sequence and marks the three moments where first-timers tend to stop: the counter, the locker room, and the first step into the bath hall.

Finding the door

Use Naver Map or KakaoMap and search in Korean script: type 목욕탕 (mogyoktang, bathhouse), 사우나 (sauna) or 찜질방, and read the Korean hours line. By law the street sign must show the registered name and the type of business, so one of those words is usually on it, often with 24시 (24-hour) in front. Several of the Seoul places in our directory are in a basement or on an upper floor of an ordinary commercial building, so look for a sign by a staircase or elevator rather than a storefront. A door marked 남탕 (namtang, men's bath) or 여탕 (yeotang, women's bath) means you have found the right floor.

Many large jjimjilbang are open 24 hours but close each bath for cleaning for part of the night: at Sparex Dongdaemun each bath closes for about an hour, and scrubs stop from 01:00 to 03:00. Neighborhood baths close in the evening; those in our directory close between 22:00 and midnight. The bath types guide explains each word and how to read a listing.

Say it
몇 시까지 해요?
myeot sikkaji haeyo?

What time do you close?

The counter: the first freeze

At many places you put your shoes in a small shoe locker (신발장, sinbaljang) by the entrance, take its key to the counter, pay, and hand the shoe key over. In exchange you get a changing-room locker key, a towel and, if there is a jjimjil hall, a cotton outfit. At other places you pay first and collect the outfit, towel and key before you put your shoes away. Sparex has a ticket kiosk with a language selector. If you are unsure, point at the price board.

Say it
어른 한 명이요
eoreun han myeong-iyo

One adult, please. Where there is both a bath and a jjimjil hall, you may be asked which ticket you want; the hall ticket costs more.

The price board (목욕요금표) must be posted at the counter by law. For orientation: the average adult fee at a general public bath in Seoul was ₩11,000 in July 2026. In 2026 listings, adult tickets at Seoul's large 24-hour jjimjilbang cost about ₩11,000–16,000, and ₩20,000 at a women-only one. The 24-hour places sell a day ticket and a night ticket. The night ticket starts at 20:00 or 21:00 and costs ₩1,000–3,000 more. Destination spas charge more: Spa Land Centum City in Busan was ₩25,000 for four hours in September 2026. The prices and hours guide has detail by city and type.

Some older places want cash, at least at the snack bar, so carry some cash and ask 카드 돼요? (kadeu dwaeyo?, do you take cards?). Bring your passport or ID: some places ask for it, and one Korean guide warns that entry can be refused without it.

The key you receive is the one object to protect. At large facilities it is a wristband that opens your locker and also works as a wallet. Scrubs and snacks go on it and are settled when you leave. Sparex Dongdaemun charges ₩20,000 for a lost key (2026). Keep it on your wrist or ankle from now on.

What you receive Large jjimjilbang Neighborhood mogyoktang
Towels Small towels, included A towel from the counter
Outfit (찜질복) Included with a hall ticket None; there is no hall
Soap and shampoo Body wash, often shampoo Often soap only; bring shampoo
Key Wristband that is also your tab Locker key on a band

The locker room: the second freeze

Look for 남 (men) or 여 (women) on the doors and find the locker with your number. Then undress completely. Nothing is worn in the bath hall: not a swimsuit, not underwear. Everyone else in the room is naked too. A small towel held in front of you while you walk is fine, as long as it never goes into the water.

Your phone stays in the locker. Phones and cameras are banned in locker and bath areas, and filming in a changing room is a sex crime in Korea: police arrested a man on the spot for it in a Daejeon bathhouse in 2023. Tie long hair up. Take only the small towel and your toiletries through the door. Walk slowly: changing-room scales, sinks and water dispensers are where slips cluster, and none of 16 Seoul bathhouses surveyed in 2025 had anti-slip mats at those spots.

Say it
사물함이 안 열려요
samulhami an yeollyeoyo

My locker won't open. Say it at the counter, or to the attendant inside.

The bath hall: the third freeze

You step into a tiled room with low shower stations along the walls, pools (tang, 탕) in the middle, a sauna, and a scrub table in a corner. Signs over the pools read 온탕 (ontang, warm, about 38–40°C), 열탕 (yeoltang, hot, above 42°C) and 냉탕 (naengtang, cold, around 15°C). Those are guide figures, not posted standards. By law, each sauna has a thermometer inside, so read it.

The rule that matters most comes first. Sit on a plastic stool at a shower station, wash your whole body with soap, and rinse completely. Only then enter a pool. Elderly regulars often correct people who skip this. A plastic bowl (bagaji, 바가지) at the station is for rinsing. You will wash again at the end.

Tip

Everything else in the hall is forgiving. Washing first is not. Do it slowly and completely.

Soak, sweat, cool: a sensible first cycle

Start in the warm pool, not the hot one. After two bathhouse deaths in May 2026, Korea's fire authorities advised a short shower and light stretching to warm up, then soaks of no more than 10–15 minutes with rest after each. Keep your heart above the waterline. If you feel dizzy, get out and sit down. The same advice warns of blood-pressure spikes when you switch between hot and cold pools, and tells people with chronic illness to avoid high-heat saunas. If that is you, read the health guide first.

Then try the sauna. The cold pool is optional. A Busan bath owner gives regulars this routine: 5–15 minutes in the sauna, one minute in the cold pool, about ten minutes of rest, three rounds. Drink water between rounds. The towel some people fold on their heads is the yangmeori (양머리, "sheep head"); the rooms guide explains it, along with every pool and room.

Dry off before you walk anywhere. Of 1,790 bathhouse injuries reported in Korea from 2021 to mid-2025, 89.3% were slips, and the main cause was people walking around wet.

Say it
물 온도 딱 좋네요
mul ondo ttak jonneyo

The water's just right.

Optional: seshin

The scrub table is where a professional of your own sex (the law forbids opposite-sex attendants in the bath) scrubs you head to toe with an Italy towel (이태리타월). This is seshin (세신). Soak first so the skin softens. Then ask at the table, or point at the service board and make a scrubbing motion. Prices at ordinary bathhouses were about ₩20,000–40,000 in 2025, charged to your wristband. If you want it gentler, say so. The rest, including tipping and who should skip it, is in the seshin guide.

Say it
살살 해주세요
salsal haejuseyo

Gently, please.

Rinse, dry, and the jjimjil hall

Rinse off at a shower station, then dry yourself completely before you step into the locker room. Hair dryers are usually provided.

If your ticket includes the jjimjil hall, put on the cotton outfit now. Korean law requires it in every mixed-gender sweat room. The hall, shared by men and women, has heated rooms of clay or salt at roughly 35–50°C, an ice room at 10–15°C, warm floors to lie on, wooden block pillows, a snack counter and, at 24-hour places, a sleeping area. Guides suggest 10–20 minutes per heated room. Do not fall asleep in a hot room, and leave if you feel dizzy. The usual order at the snack counter is a cold can of sikhye (식혜, sweet rice drink) and stone-baked eggs (맥반석 계란), each about ₩1,000–3,000 in early 2026, charged to the wristband. Keep your voice down, as in the bath.

Say it
식혜 하나 주세요
sikhye hana juseyo

One sikhye, please.

You can go back to the bath afterward. Mind the clock where there is a time limit: Spa Land's ticket covers four hours and charges extra after that, and Sparex charges extra once you pass 24 hours. The food and overnight guides cover the hall in detail.

The counter again: checkout

Return the outfit and towels, hand over the wristband, and pay whatever is on it. Collect your shoe key, or open the shoe locker with the same key. Most facilities do not allow re-entry once you leave; Sparex sells one re-entry as part of a luggage-storage ticket. Say 감사합니다 (gamsahamnida, thank you) on the way out.

Say it
또 올게요
tto olgeyo

I'll come again.

Before you go, read the unwritten rules once, and if you have tattoos, the tattoo guide.
