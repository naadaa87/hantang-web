It enters Korea's royal annals in 1422 as a sweat house run by monks, because patients were dying there. What the records say, and what the word means on a sign today. tag: CULTURE ---

A hanjeungmak (한증막) is a dome of stone and clay, heated by a wood fire and then used for sweating. Among the rooms of a jjimjilbang (찜질방) it has the longest paper trail. It enters Korea's royal annals in 1422, and not as good news: sick people were dying in sweat houses run by monks, and King Sejong wanted to know whether the treatment worked.

What the word means

Hanjeung (한증, 汗蒸) is written with the characters for "sweat" and "steam". The Encyclopedia of Korean Culture defines it as warming the body in a purpose-built room to bring on a sweat, for treatment or for health. Mak (幕) is a hut. The Joseon records say hanjeungso (한증소), "sweat house".

Bulgama (불가마, "fire kiln") is what many jjimjilbang call their hottest room. A sutgama (숯가마) is a working charcoal kiln, opened for sweating once the charcoal is out. Our kiln sauna list separates fire domes from charcoal kilns.

1422: the first record is a complaint

The word first appears in the Annals of King Sejong. We read the entries through the Encyclopedia of Korean Culture, the Academy of Korean Studies' dictionary of the Annals and a 2018 medical-history paper, not in the original.

YearWhat the record says
1422, 8th lunar monthSick people who went to a sweat house sometimes died. The king orders an inquiry into whether sweating helps.
1422, 10th lunar monthOfficials report that the monks make everyone sweat without asking what is wrong. The king keeps the sweat houses at the two state relief clinics, the East and West Hwarinwon (동서활인원), and inside the capital, but sends physicians to examine patients first. Monk and physician both answer for a death from careless diagnosis.
1427–1429The monks receive rice and cotton cloth as an endowment for treating poor patients, then ask to build three more rooms, separating bathers by rank and sex.
1445A temple in a residential quarter asks to repair its sweat house. The court closes the temple and gives its equipment and endowment to the two clinics.

So the sweat house began as a clinic, not a place to rest, and its first rule was a safety rule: ask what is wrong before the heat.

The year 1422 is a first record, not an invention date. The 2018 paper argues that monks were sweating patients long before, in a practice that came with Buddhism, though it admits no earlier Korean record survives.

How the kiln worked

By the encyclopedia's account, pine branches were burned in the middle of the floor of a closed room, with air fed in through a buried clay pipe. When the room was hot enough, water was thrown on the fire and the ash raked out. Wet straw mats went down, in earlier times with pine branches on top. Bathers went in wrapped in cloth or a straw sack, lay down for four or five minutes, came out to cool and went back in.

A survey from the 1920s, quoted in the 2018 paper, found the building much the same everywhere: stones stacked into a dome and plastered with earth. The paper lists sweat kilns from Hamhung in the north to Busan in the south.

In Gaeseong in the 1920s, the paper says, men and women of every class sweated at least every other day, five to ten at a time. Inside, they counted aloud to a hundred, adding the name of the bodhisattva Gwaneum to each number. Afterwards they scrubbed, rinsed with cold water and drank barley tea in a rest room.

An old kiln that still stands

A stone hanjeungmak from the late Joseon period survives at Gogu-ri on Gyodong Island, in Ganghwa County, Incheon. The dictionary of the Annals calls it the largest remaining example, about three meters high: a mound of clay and stones around a hollow. A 2015 Seoul Shinmun travel report places it on the western slope of Mount Hwagae and passes on a local guide's estimate that it was used now and then until the 1960s. We have not confirmed how to reach it today.

The word on signs today

Today a hanjeungmak is usually one room inside a jjimjilbang, as our guide to bath types explains, but the word is still on many signs. Our nationwide list, built from government licensing data downloaded on October 31, 2026, holds 5,640 places marked as operating. In it, 158 have 한증막 in their name, 35 in Seoul. Eighty-three write it as 불한증막, "fire hanjeungmak". Across the list, 87 names contain 불가마 and 51 contain 숯가마 (our count, November 24, 2026). A name does not prove a wood fire, and many kilns are not on the sign.

Published temperatures disagree; our rooms guide compares them. A 1994 study describes a kiln that cooled to about 80°C over the twelve hours after firing. In 2001 a professor of rehabilitation medicine told the Seoul Shinmun to stay in a hot room no more than ten minutes at a time, and thirty minutes in total.

If you try one

  • Dress for it. Wear the outfit or gown you are given and cover your head. The 2001 article warns of burns to the skin and eyes.
  • Keep it short. Go in briefly, come out, cool down and repeat, as people did in the 15th century. Never fall asleep inside.
  • Know who should skip it. The 1994 study warns of heat stroke and of the risk to people with heart disease. If you are pregnant, have a heart or blood-pressure condition, or have been drinking, read our health and safety guide first.
