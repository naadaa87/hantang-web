title: Why is Korea's scrub mitt called the Italy towel? description: The rough green mitt in every Korean bathhouse was first made in Busan in the 1960s, from rayon cloth brought in from Italy. What the records say about its name and inventor, and how to use one. tag: CULTURE ---

The Italy towel (이태리타월, itaeri tawol) is the small, rough mitt Koreans use to scrub off ttae (때), the dead skin that rolls up after a long soak. It is a Korean product. It was first made in Busan in the 1960s, and the usual explanation for the name is that the cloth, a viscose rayon, was imported from Italy. As the Korea Times pointed out in 2008, you will not find an Italy towel in Italy.

What it is

A 2003 article in the Korea JoongAng Daily describes an abrasive cloth about 15 centimeters square, sewn on three sides to make a pouch for the hand, in bright green, red or yellow. Green is the one most people picture. You may read that each color marks a grade of roughness. We could not confirm a standard that all makers follow, so judge by touch, not by color.

Korean dictionaries spell the word 이태리타월, and shops often write 이태리타올. Itaeri is an older Korean name for Italy. The mitt is also called ttaesugeon (때수건), "ttae towel".

Why "Italy"

Two versions are in print, and both lead to Busan in the 1960s.

The cloth came from Italy. A 2018 column in the Korean daily Financial News says a Busan company, Hanil Textile (한일직물), imported viscose rayon cloth from Italy to make a new kind of towel. The cloth was too rough to dry yourself with, but in the bathhouse that roughness turned out to be useful. The Korea Times agrees on the material: the first mitts sold in Busan were 100 percent viscose rayon from Italy.

The loom came from Italy. In the 2003 JoongAng article, a spokesman for a Korean towel maker said a hotel owner in Busan came up with the idea, using cloth woven on a machine imported from Italy. The article itself admits that "no one seems to know for certain."

Who invented it, and when

The Korean press usually names Kim Pil-gon (김필곤, died 2001) of Hanil Textile. The Financial News column that names him also says the credit is disputed, without saying by whom. The dates do not agree either.

We have not searched the patent register ourselves. What the sources share is narrower: Busan, the 1960s, and a rough rayon cloth. The 2018 column adds that the utility models have all lapsed, that four trademarks on the name were still in force that year, and that much of the production had moved to China.

From a Busan product to a national habit

Scrubbing is older than the mitt. The head of a bathhouse owners' association told the JoongAng Daily in 2003 that people used dried straw or stones before it.

In 1981 a workshop in Busan built a coin-operated back scrubber, the Weekly Kyunghyang reports. A 100-won coin ran the motor for a few minutes, and a round head wrapped in Italy towel did the work. The machine spread to Seoul and Daegu and then mostly disappeared. In 2020 the ones that survived were almost all in Busan and South Gyeongsang, so the Busan bathhouse list is the place to start if you want to see one. We have not checked which baths still have one.

In 2008 the Korea Times put annual sales at more than 10 billion won, at about 1,000 won a piece, with exports to Japan since the early 1990s. In 2017 the Korean Intellectual Property Office asked its Facebook followers to rate 25 Korean inventions chosen by experts. The Italy towel came sixth. The Korean alphabet came first, and the kimchi refrigerator seventh.

How to use one

  • Soak first. Both English-language articles give the same figure: at least 15 minutes in warm water, so the skin softens.
  • Scrub at your shower station. Slip your hand into the pouch and scrub on your stool, away from the pools. Rinse completely before you go back in the water.
  • Go easy on the face and neck. The 2003 article warns that the skin there is sensitive.
  • Do not do it often. Dermatologists quoted in our seshin guide advise only an occasional, gentle scrub, and list the skin and health conditions that rule it out.

How people hold it differs. A 2020 Segye Ilbo report on the folk museum's bathhouse study says men tend to wrap the cloth around a hand towel, while women use a glove type. If you would rather have someone else do it, the same guide explains seshin (세신), the professional scrub.

Where to buy one

The simplest place is the bathhouse counter. The neighborhood bath in the Segye Ilbo report sold them at the ticket window.

It has also become a souvenir. In July 2026 Newsis visited bath-goods shops at Namdaemun Market in Seoul, where the mitts are displayed in many colors. A shopkeeper said Japanese, Chinese and Western tourists all buy them, and one visitor explained why: they are cheap and small enough to pack. We did not confirm a 2026 price.
