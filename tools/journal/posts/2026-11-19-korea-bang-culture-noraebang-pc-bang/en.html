title: Noraebang, PC bang, jjimjilbang: what is Korea's bang culture? description: Bang (방) means room. A noraebang or PC bang rents you a room or a seat by the hour; a jjimjilbang sells one ticket to a shared hall. Where each came from, how many are left, and which ones a traveler can try. tag: KOREA ---

Bang (방) means room. At the end of a word it names a business that rents you a room, or a seat in one, for a set time: the noraebang (노래방) for singing, the PC bang (PC방) for games, the manhwabang (만화방) for comics. Writers in Korea call the pattern bang culture (방 문화). The jjimjilbang (찜질방) carries the same word but works the other way round: you buy one ticket and share a large heated hall with strangers.

The bang is older than the 1990s

Two older rooms came first. The dabang (다방), a tearoom, sold coffee and a seat to talk in. The Encyclopedia of Korean Culture, published by the Academy of Korean Studies, dates the first dabang run by a Korean to 1927 in Seoul and counts about 45,000 at the peak in 1992. Coffee shops then took over, and in 1997 the dabang owners' association dropped the word from its name.

The manhwabang, a shop where you pay to read or borrow comics, appeared in the 1950s. The same encyclopedia cites newspaper surveys from the mid-1960s that counted about 1,000 in Seoul and 4,500 nationwide, at one or two won a book.

1991: the singing room

The noraebang started in Busan. In April 1991 the owner of an arcade next to Dong-A University, in the Hadan-dong neighborhood, rebuilt a karaoke machine so customers could pick songs by number and set it up in a small booth. A song cost 300 won, according to Busan Ilbo and JoongAng Ilbo. JoongAng adds that no official record exists; the industry treats this arcade as the first. The first registered singing room opened a month later in the city's Gwangalli district, and by December Busan had about 200.

Why it spread is a matter of opinion. A Busan Museum researcher told Busan Ilbo in 2019 that the first singing rooms sold no alcohol, so they drew a wider public than the karaoke bars of the time. A cultural studies professor told the Korea JoongAng Daily in 2011 that young people then had few places to go that were not about drinking. The count peaked at 37,568 in 2005, by interior ministry figures. The National Tax Service counted 27,779 in November 2021.

The PC bang and StarCraft

PC bangs, rooms of fast computers rented by the hour, date from the 1990s. StarCraft went on sale in Korea in April 1998, and the daily Money Today credits the game with spreading them nationwide. The Korea Creative Content Agency puts the 2001 count at 22,548. The National Tax Service, which counts differently, had 10,568 in 2017 and 7,310 in 2024. Reports name powerful home computers and mobile games as the reasons. In August 2023 The Korea Herald put the usual rate at ₩1,000–1,500 an hour and said much of the income now comes from snacks and drinks.

Why rooms?

There is no measured answer, only readings. A July 2026 column in The Korea Times, by an associate professor at Vietnam National University Ho Chi Minh City, ties the rooms to dense cities, apartment living and limited private space: people buy an hour of privacy outside the home. The column's phrase for it is "scarcity becomes infrastructure."

The rooms are also changing. In February 2022 JoongAng Ilbo wrote of the fall of "bang and night culture." Pandemic curfews hit hard, but the paper noted that after-work gatherings had been thinning before that, and a sociologist it quoted pointed to younger people who prefer to do things alone. The coin noraebang (코인노래방) fits that reading. It is a booth you pay for by the song, and many customers sing alone. In 2018 the Korea JoongAng Daily reported a typical rate of 1,000 won for three or four songs. We did not confirm 2026 prices.

The hours for minors come from the youth-protection pages of two Seoul district offices and a 2024 explainer by the Ministry of Government Legislation. The district lists also close video and DVD rooms to under-19s at any hour.

In a noraebang the door shuts and the room is yours. A jjimjilbang hall cannot work that way. As our guide to bath types explains, the law requires sweat rooms and rest rooms to be visible from outside and does not allow closed private rooms. What you buy is a place among other people: men and women, families and people on their own, on one heated floor in the same cotton outfit. The jjimjilbang became popular in the 1990s, the same decade as the other two, and a January 2020 count found about 1,300. Our short history of the Korean bath has the longer story.

What a traveler can try

  • Coin noraebang. You do not need a group. You pay for a few songs at a time and choose them by number.
  • PC bang. You rent a seat by the hour, and food and drinks are sold inside. We did not confirm how visitors without a Korean phone number sign in, so ask at the counter first.
  • Jjimjilbang. The least private of the three and the longest stay. At a 24-hour place you can sleep in the hall, which the overnight guide covers.

Traveling with teenagers? Check the last column of the table before planning a late evening. For the jjimjilbang, read the first-visit guide, then look through bathhouses near popular areas.
