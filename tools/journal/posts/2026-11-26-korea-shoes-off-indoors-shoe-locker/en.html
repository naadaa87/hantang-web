title: Shoes off in Korea: why, where, and the bathhouse shoe locker description: Koreans take their shoes off at home because the floor is heated and lived on. Travelers meet the same custom at restaurants with floor seating and at the bathhouse, where a shoe locker comes before the changing room. tag: KOREA ---

In Korea shoes come off at the door of a home, and at some businesses too: restaurants with floor seating, and bathhouses. The reason is the floor. Korean houses have long been heated from below, and people sat, ate and slept on the floor. That is why the first thing you meet at a mogyoktang (목욕탕) or a jjimjilbang (찜질방) is a wall of shoe lockers.

The heated floor is the reason

Ondol (온돌) is heating that warms the floor, and the floor then warms the room. The Encyclopedia of Korean Culture, published by the Academy of Korean Studies, calls it a main cause of a home life in which people take off their shoes and sit or lie on the floor. By its account, ondol spread fastest in the 17th century, across regions and classes. Ondol culture became national intangible heritage on April 30, 2018.

The encyclopedia notes that hot-water pipes or electric panels have since replaced the old flues under the floor. In August 2026 The Korea Herald wrote, in a guide to visiting a Korean home, that people who own chairs, sofas and beds still often spend time on the floor. A jjimjilbang hall works the same way: guests sit, nap and, at 24-hour places, sleep on a heated floor.

At a home, a guesthouse or a hanok stay

The Korea Herald puts the house rule plainly: no shoes inside, and residents and guests alike leave them near the door. Soft indoor slippers are becoming more common, it adds, to keep footsteps quiet for the neighbors downstairs. They are not the rubber slippers kept in the bathroom, which stay there.

In a traditional house, a hanok (한옥), the way in is a step up: the encyclopedia describes stepping from a stone onto a narrow wooden veranda, the toenmaru (툇마루). At a guesthouse or a hanok stay, look for the cues: shoes lined up by the door, a shoe shelf, slippers set out, or a raised floor. If unsure, ask 신발 벗어야 돼요? (sinbal beoseoya dwaeyo?), "Should I take my shoes off?"

Restaurants: floor seating is getting rarer

Outside the home the custom applies, in the words of a 2024 Korea Times article, at "some businesses such as restaurants with floor seating." Jwasik (좌식) is sitting on the floor at a low table, and ipsik (입식) is tables and chairs. You take your shoes off only for the first.

Floor seating is losing ground. In February 2018 the same paper reported that a growing number of restaurants had replaced it with chairs. A staff member at one said sitting on the floor causes back and knee pain, and an office worker said she did not want to take off her shoes. Some cities have paid part of the cost: in March 2022 Jeonju offered 20 restaurants up to ₩7 million each for improvements that included replacing floor tables, the Jeonbuk Ilbo reported. We found no national count. If you would rather keep your shoes on, ask for a table with chairs.

The sign that says lost shoes are your problem

A shoe shelf may carry a notice such as 신발 분실 시 책임지지 않습니다, "we are not responsible for lost shoes." Korean law does not take the sign at its word. Article 152 of the Commercial Act (상법) covers businesses that serve the public on their premises, and a Korean legal reference site lists bathhouses among them. The business is liable when something a customer brought in is lost through the fault of the owner or staff, and a notice denying responsibility does not remove that liability. We read the article on private legal sites, not in the official gazette.

In 2016 the Seoul Shinmun described a diner whose new shoes disappeared from a restaurant shelf with such a sign. The Korea Consumer Agency (한국소비자원), cited in the article, said the customer can ask the owner for compensation. The share, the paper wrote, depends on the care the restaurant took: lockable shelves, shoe bags, cameras. On a trip, prevention is easier: use a locker with a key where there is one, and do not wear your best pair to a floor-seating dinner.

At the bathhouse, the shoe locker comes first

Nobody walks into a Korean bathhouse in shoes. The shoe locker (신발장, sinbaljang) stands by the entrance. At many places you lock your shoes in, take the key to the counter, pay, and hand that key over in exchange for the key or wristband to your changing-room locker. At others you pay first. You may hear 신발 사물함 열쇠 받으세요, "take your shoe-locker key." When you leave, you settle the bill and collect your shoes. Our first-visit guide goes through each step, and the phrase guide shows the signs.

Guard the key: our guide notes that Sparex Dongdaemun charges ₩20,000 for a lost one (2026). Our health guide cites a Korea Consumer Agency count of 1,790 bathhouse injuries from 2021 to June 2025, 89.3% of them slips and falls, so dry off before you walk. It also passes on dermatologists' advice to bring your own slippers, because athlete's foot spreads through shared ones.
